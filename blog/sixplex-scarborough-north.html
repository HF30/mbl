Can You Build a Sixplex in Scarborough North? Ward 23

Scarborough North (Ward 23) is one of the nine Toronto wards where up to six residential units are permitted as-of-right, under Zoning By-law 654-2025 and Official Plan Amendment 818 (June 2025). Being in the ward gets a lot to the starting line. Whether six units actually fit — and whether the project pencils — is decided by the specific lot, not the ward.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments · one of a nine-part series, one post per ward.

What the ward covers

Northeast Scarborough. Bounded by Steeles Avenue East (the Toronto–Markham city limit) to the north, Highway 401 to the south, roughly Midland Avenue to the west, and the Rouge River / Morningside–Neilson area to the east. It is the only fully suburban ward among the nine sixplex wards. The ward takes in the northeast corner of Scarborough along the Steeles and Finch corridors, including Agincourt North, Milliken and Steeles, extending toward Malvern in the east, with L'Amoreaux adjacent to the south. Ward lines follow specific streets and get redrawn from time to time, so treat any neighbourhood list as a guide, not a survey — the City publishes the official ward boundaries on toronto.ca.

Neighbourhoods & built form

The character changes street to street, and the built form is what matters most for a sixplex — it’s the raw material you’re working with, and the heritage-character pockets are where the “does it fit” question gets hardest.

  • Agincourt North. Postwar detached brick bungalows and two-storey homes built largely in the 1960s–1980s, on wider suburban lots, interspersed with townhouse and apartment clusters. The larger typical lot sizes here can be an advantage for a sixplex footprint compared with narrow downtown lots.
  • Milliken. Former farmland urbanized mainly in the 1980s; detached houses on generous lots plus townhouse and mid/high-rise apartment clusters near Steeles and Kennedy. Home to a large, long-established Chinese-Canadian community and anchored by Milliken District Park and its community recreation centre.
  • Steeles. City neighbourhood along the Steeles/Finch corridor east of Kennedy; a mix of detached and semi-detached homes, townhouse blocks and apartment towers, with retail plazas fronting the arterials.
  • Malvern (western edge). Planned 1970s–80s suburban subdivision of detached and semi-detached homes with townhouse clusters and some apartment sites; large, regular lots typical of master-planned Scarborough.

Getting around

No subway serves this ward — an honest point of difference from the downtown sixplex wards. Transit is bus-and-GO based: TTC surface routes run along Finch, Steeles (e.g. 53 Steeles East), Kennedy and McCowan, feeding Line 4 (Sheppard) and Line 1 stations to the south. Two GO Transit stations on the Stouffville line, Milliken and Agincourt, sit at the ward's edge and connect to Union Station, with a new Finch–Kennedy infill station planned between them. Highway access is via Highway 401 to the south and Highway 404 to the west; car ownership is high.

Downtown access & lifestyle

Suburban and amenity-rich in a plaza-and-mall sense rather than a walkable main-street sense. Large multicultural retail defines the area: the Splendid China / former Market Village complex sits at Steeles and Kennedy, with Pacific Mall just across Steeles in Markham, and big-box plazas line the arterials. Green space includes Milliken District Park (32 hectares) with its community recreation centre, which hosts a City fireworks show each Canada Day. The downtown core is far — a GO-train or long transit ride away — so this is not a downtown-adjacent location; rental demand is driven instead by local families, newcomers, and proximity to suburban jobs, schools and the GO line.

Schools & families

Served by the Toronto District School Board (TDSB) and Toronto Catholic District School Board (TCDSB), with placement by home-address catchment; both boards operate elementary and secondary schools across the ward. No single standout school or EQAO result is asserted here as verified, so this is a neutral note — confirm the specific catchment for any address rather than rely on a ward-wide claim.

Who lives here

An established, family-oriented and comparatively older suburban population. Median age is 42.8 vs 39.6 citywide, and seniors make up 20% of residents vs 17% for Toronto, while the 30–39 group is under-represented (13.0% vs 16.6%). The ward has a large immigrant population, with a notably strong East and South Asian presence — Milliken in particular has a long-established Chinese-Canadian community — reflected in the area's multicultural retail and community institutions.

What that means for a sixplex — and what it doesn’t

A ward that is mostly ground-related, older housing is, in the abstract, the kind of place the six-unit framework was written for: detached, semi-detached, and row houses on residential lots. That is the raw material. It is also exactly where the real questions begin, because none of the ward-level numbers above can tell you what a specific lot will carry.

Being in Ward 23 means the six-unit framework may apply to a lot here. It does not mean six units fit, or that the building is otherwise compliant or pencils. The same lot-by-lot gates apply here as everywhere:

  • Lot geometry. Frontage and depth, minus setbacks, set the buildable envelope. On a narrow lot the sixth unit often has to become a rear suite — a different legal and financing object than six units in one building.
  • Overlays, heritage, and specific zoning. The ward is the boundary; the actual zoning, heritage designations, and overlays run lot by lot and can quietly cap what you build.
  • Trees, easements, servicing. A protected tree, a registered easement, or a servicing constraint can constrain the design, add cost, or reduce the unit count — long before price is the issue.
  • What’s in the ground. On any lot with an existing or former structure, environmental status is unknown until it’s investigated. Real cost, real gate.

A qualified zoning review on the specific lot is step one, and if the review and the ward map ever disagree, the review wins.

How you’d actually evaluate a lot here

The honest sequence is the same in every one of the nine wards: confirm the lot can carry six units on paper (geometry), confirm the market supports the rents (closed-lease evidence, not asking prices), and confirm the numbers pencil at a price you can actually pay — before you fall in love with an address. It runs lot by lot, not ward by ward. That discipline is the whole difference between a buildable sixplex and an expensive lesson.

Can You Build a Sixplex in Toronto–Danforth? Ward 14

Toronto–Danforth (Ward 14) is one of the nine Toronto wards where up to six residential units are permitted as-of-right, under Zoning By-law 654-2025 and Official Plan Amendment 818 (June 2025). Being in the ward gets a lot to the starting line. Whether six units actually fit — and whether the project pencils — is decided by the specific lot, not the ward.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments · one of a nine-part series, one post per ward.

What the ward covers

Ward 14 runs from the Don Valley Parkway east to Coxwell Avenue, and from Lake Ontario north to just above O’Connor Drive, taking in Riverdale, Riverside, Leslieville, Greektown / the Danforth, Playter Estates, the Gerrard India Bazaar and East Chinatown. Ward lines follow specific streets and get redrawn from time to time, so treat any neighbourhood list as a guide, not a survey — the City publishes the official ward boundaries on toronto.ca.

Neighbourhoods & built form

The character changes street to street, and the built form is what matters most for a sixplex — it’s the raw material you’re working with, and the heritage-character pockets are where the “does it fit” question gets hardest.

  • Riverdale. Victorian and Edwardian semis and detached homes in brick — classic bay-and-gable stock — on the slope west toward the Don Valley.
  • Playter Estates. A small early-1900s enclave of Edwardian and Tudor houses with wide porches, north of the Danforth; a recognized heritage-character pocket.
  • Leslieville & Riverside. Former industrial blocks and Victorian workers’ cottages, now a mix of infill and retail along Queen Street East.
  • Greektown / the Danforth. The commercial spine along Danforth Avenue, with residential streets running back off it.
  • Gerrard India Bazaar & East Chinatown. Established cultural retail strips that anchor the ward’s eastern and southern edges.

Getting around

Line 2 (Bloor–Danforth) runs the length of the Danforth — Broadview, Chester, Pape, Donlands, Greenwood and Coxwell stations sit in or along the ward. The 504/505 streetcars cross the south end, Danforth GO is on the Lakeshore East line, and the planned Ontario Line is set to add stations toward Greektown and Leslieville. The Don Valley Parkway runs the western edge. Transit access like this is a large part of why the ward’s rental demand holds up — and why the corridor is intensifying.

Downtown access & lifestyle

Ward 14 sits right against the downtown core — just across the Don River — so the financial district is a short subway or Don Valley Parkway trip, and much of the ward is genuinely walk- or bike-to-work close. That access, paired with the amenities, is a big part of why the east end leases. The Danforth is one of Toronto’s best-known restaurant and nightlife strips — its Greektown stretch hosts the Taste of the Danforth, among the country’s largest street festivals — while Leslieville and Queen Street East have built a reputation for independent cafés, restaurants and design shops. Riverdale and Withrow parks give the family side of the demand somewhere to land, and the south end of the ward opens onto the lake at Ashbridges Bay Park — beach, boardwalk and the Martin Goodman Trail — with the Port Lands and the Leslie Street Spit close by. (The Beach village proper, around Queen and Woodbine, sits just east of Coxwell in Ward 19.) For a rental operator, walkable, transit-rich, amenity-dense neighbourhoods with water and parks at the edge are the ones that lease quickly and keep tenants.

Schools & families

The Danforth and Riverdale are a long-standing family draw, and the public schools are a real part of it. Schools here tend to sit among the city’s stronger performers on provincial EQAO testing — Riverdale is regularly grouped with the Beaches and North Toronto on that measure. Well-known local options include Jackman Avenue Public School (with an English and early-French-immersion stream), Riverdale Collegiate, and Danforth Technical, which runs a math, science and technology focus. Rankings move year to year and are assigned by catchment, not neighbourhood, so confirm the specific school for any address on the TDSB and TCDSB locators. For a sixplex, that family demand is part of what keeps larger, three-bedroom units leased.

Who lives here

The ward skews to established and family-forming households rather than a young, transient crowd. The median age is 40.8 (a shade above the city), the largest cohorts are people in their thirties and forties — roughly a third of residents — and there are proportionally more young children than the Toronto average, while the twenty-something share actually runs below the city. Read as a rental market, that points to demand for larger, longer-tenancy homes — the family-sized two- and three-bedroom suites a sixplex is well-placed to add — rather than a churn of small studios.

What that means for a sixplex — and what it doesn’t

A ward that is mostly ground-related, older housing is, in the abstract, the kind of place the six-unit framework was written for: detached, semi-detached, and row houses on residential lots. That is the raw material. It is also exactly where the real questions begin, because none of the ward-level numbers above can tell you what a specific lot will carry.

Being in Ward 14 means the six-unit framework may apply to a lot here. It does not mean six units fit, or that the building is otherwise compliant or pencils. The same lot-by-lot gates apply here as everywhere:

  • Lot geometry. Frontage and depth, minus setbacks, set the buildable envelope. On a narrow lot the sixth unit often has to become a rear suite — a different legal and financing object than six units in one building.
  • Overlays, heritage, and specific zoning. The ward is the boundary; the actual zoning, heritage designations, and overlays run lot by lot and can quietly cap what you build.
  • Trees, easements, servicing. A protected tree, a registered easement, or a servicing constraint can constrain the design, add cost, or reduce the unit count — long before price is the issue.
  • What’s in the ground. On any lot with an existing or former structure, environmental status is unknown until it’s investigated. Real cost, real gate.

A qualified zoning review on the specific lot is step one, and if the review and the ward map ever disagree, the review wins.

How you’d actually evaluate a lot here

The honest sequence is the same in every one of the nine wards: confirm the lot can carry six units on paper (geometry), confirm the market supports the rents (closed-lease evidence, not asking prices), and confirm the numbers pencil at a price you can actually pay — before you fall in love with an address. It runs lot by lot, not ward by ward. That discipline is the whole difference between a buildable sixplex and an expensive lesson.

Can You Build a Sixplex in Beaches–East York? Ward 19

Beaches–East York (Ward 19) is one of the nine Toronto wards where up to six residential units are permitted as-of-right, under Zoning By-law 654-2025 and Official Plan Amendment 818 (June 2025). Being in the ward gets a lot to the starting line. Whether six units actually fit — and whether the project pencils — is decided by the specific lot, not the ward.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments · one of a nine-part series, one post per ward.

What the ward covers

Ward 19 sits in Toronto's east end, bounded by Lake Ontario to the south, Coxwell Avenue to the west, Victoria Park Avenue to the east, and an irregular northern edge around O'Connor Drive. The ward lies east of Coxwell. The ward takes in the Beach/Beaches lakeside strip around Queen Street East and Woodbine (including Kew Beach), the Upper Beaches north of Kingston Road, Danforth East / East Danforth toward Main Street, the Woodbine-Lumsden / Woodbine Heights area of old East York, and the Crescent Town / Taylor-Massey apartment district near Victoria Park and Danforth. Ward lines follow specific streets and get redrawn from time to time, so treat any neighbourhood list as a guide, not a survey — the City publishes the official ward boundaries on toronto.ca.

Neighbourhoods & built form

The character changes street to street, and the built form is what matters most for a sixplex — it’s the raw material you’re working with, and the heritage-character pockets are where the “does it fit” question gets hardest.

  • The Beach / Kew Beach. Lakeside grid around Queen East and Woodbine; predominantly early-1900s Edwardian and Victorian brick houses, semi-detached rows and some former summer cottages on narrow lots — pre-1960 fabric consistent with the ward's construction skew.
  • Upper Beaches. North of Kingston Road; smaller detached houses and semis on modest lots, largely pre-war ground-related stock.
  • Danforth East / East Danforth. Along Danforth Avenue toward Main Street; mix of two-storey semis and detached houses behind a commercial main street, with scattered newer mid-rise near Main Street and Danforth GO.
  • Woodbine-Lumsden / Woodbine Heights. Old East York; 1940s–1950s brick bungalows and semi-detached houses on regular lots.
  • Crescent Town / Taylor-Massey. Near Victoria Park and Danforth; a cluster of 1970s high-rise apartment towers, the main concentration of mid/high-rise dwellings in the ward.
  • Woodbine Gardens / O'Connor edge. Post-war detached and semi-detached houses on the ward's northern fringe.

Getting around

TTC Line 2 (Bloor–Danforth) runs through the north of the ward with Main Street, Woodbine and Victoria Park stations. GO Transit's Lakeshore East line serves Danforth GO near Main Street and Danforth. The 501 Queen streetcar runs east along Queen Street East into the Beach; Kingston Road and Danforth Avenue surface routes connect the residential areas to the subway.

Downtown access & lifestyle

Waterfront amenities anchor the south end: Woodbine Beach, Kew Gardens, the Boardwalk and the Martin Goodman Trail along Lake Ontario. Queen Street East through the Beach and Danforth Avenue provide retail and dining main streets. Downtown is reachable by Line 2 subway and by GO from Danforth station. The Beaches International Jazz Festival — a free festival running across July with StreetFest along Queen Street East and stages at Woodbine Park — is a notable annual event drawing large crowds. Waterfront access, main-street amenities and subway/GO connectivity support ongoing rental demand.

Schools & families

The Beaches area's TDSB elementary schools include Kew Beach Junior Public School, Balmy Beach Community School and Adam Beck Junior Public School, all in the lakeside neighbourhood. Third-party report cards (the Fraser Institute's, based on 2023–24 EQAO data) have rated Balmy Beach highly, and the Beaches is frequently grouped with areas like Riverdale and North Toronto in discussions of strong EQAO results. Rankings shift year to year and are assigned by catchment, so confirm the specific school and current scores for any address against EQAO/TDSB data.

Who lives here

Census age data indicate a family-oriented profile: children aged 0–9 are 11.5% of the ward vs 9.1% citywide, and 10–19 year-olds 10.8% vs 9.6%. Conversely 20-somethings are under-represented — 10.5% here vs 15.5% for Toronto. The ward has a higher youth dependency (17% youth vs 14% citywide) and a demographic dependency ratio of 47 dependants per 100 working-age people (Toronto 44). Median age is 41.2 vs 39.6.

What that means for a sixplex — and what it doesn’t

A ward that is mostly ground-related, older housing is, in the abstract, the kind of place the six-unit framework was written for: detached, semi-detached, and row houses on residential lots. That is the raw material. It is also exactly where the real questions begin, because none of the ward-level numbers above can tell you what a specific lot will carry.

Being in Ward 19 means the six-unit framework may apply to a lot here. It does not mean six units fit, or that the building is otherwise compliant or pencils. The same lot-by-lot gates apply here as everywhere:

  • Lot geometry. Frontage and depth, minus setbacks, set the buildable envelope. On a narrow lot the sixth unit often has to become a rear suite — a different legal and financing object than six units in one building.
  • Overlays, heritage, and specific zoning. The ward is the boundary; the actual zoning, heritage designations, and overlays run lot by lot and can quietly cap what you build.
  • Trees, easements, servicing. A protected tree, a registered easement, or a servicing constraint can constrain the design, add cost, or reduce the unit count — long before price is the issue.
  • What’s in the ground. On any lot with an existing or former structure, environmental status is unknown until it’s investigated. Real cost, real gate.

A qualified zoning review on the specific lot is step one, and if the review and the ward map ever disagree, the review wins.

How you’d actually evaluate a lot here

The honest sequence is the same in every one of the nine wards: confirm the lot can carry six units on paper (geometry), confirm the market supports the rents (closed-lease evidence, not asking prices), and confirm the numbers pencil at a price you can actually pay — before you fall in love with an address. It runs lot by lot, not ward by ward. That discipline is the whole difference between a buildable sixplex and an expensive lesson.

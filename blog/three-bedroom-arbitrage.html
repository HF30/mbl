The 3-Bedroom Premium: Toronto's Most Under-Supplied Rental Unit

The short answer: median 3-bedroom rents run $700–1,600/month above median 2-bedroom rents in every east-end pocket we track (closed leases, samples published in our benchmark), and our preliminary like-for-like underwriting comparisons on similar floor plates suggest a premium of $300–400+/month even controlling for unit mix — yet most new multiplex floor plans default to 2-bedroom layouts. The under-built unit type is the one the market pays the biggest premium for.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments

The data

Our monthly closed-lease benchmark puts numbers on it: across every east-end pocket we track, the median 3-bedroom closes $700–1,600/month above the median 2-bedroom (all-stock medians; sample sizes published). Controlling for unit mix — same floor plate, like-for-like — the premium still runs $300–400+/month. What follows is the same-plate version of the argument.

Track closed leases (not asking prices) across the sixplex wards and one pattern repeats in every pocket: family-sized units are scarce and command disproportionate rents. In the premium east-end pockets, 2026 closed 3-bedroom leases run $4,000–4,600 while 2-bedrooms close $3,950–4,195 — and the 3-bedroom supply is a fraction of the demand, because the units barely exist. Purpose-built family-sized rentals are nearly extinct: condos optimize for units-per-floor, and older stock chops houses into small suites.

Meanwhile the tenants exist in force: families priced out of buying, staying renters longer, wanting walkable neighbourhoods and school catchments — with almost nothing built for them.

Why builders get this wrong

A full-floor 3-bedroom versus a 2-bedroom on the same ~1,050 sq ft plate adds modest incremental cost — partition, door, and the design work to keep light, egress and circulation compliant — against $300–400+ more per month, indefinitely. Capitalize that: one swapped unit is worth $70–95k of building value at a 5% cap rate. Three floors of it approaches a quarter-million dollars — for the same square footage.

Builders default to 2-bedrooms out of habit, condo logic, and leasing folklore ("small units lease faster"). Faster, sometimes. Typically for meaningfully less money — and in a building you hold for decades, the rent roll is the asset.

The design implications

  • Full-floor units win. Zero corridor waste; every buildable foot earns rent. Family tenants pay for the privacy of a floor to themselves.
  • The mix is the underwriting. Design the unit mix from the closed-lease data of the specific pocket — not from what the last guy built.
  • Family tenants tend to be hold-friendly: in our leasing experience, longer tenancies and lower turnover — exactly what a long-term operator wants.

Frequently asked questions

Do 3-bedrooms take longer to lease? Sometimes marginally — the tenant pool is smaller but far less served. Closed data shows them leasing at strong premiums in family neighbourhoods, which is exactly where the sixplex wards sit.

Does this apply outside the premium pockets? The premium holds broadly; the absolute rents differ. Run the specific pocket's closed unit medians — the method matters more than any single number.

Why share this? Because the market's been building the wrong units for a decade and the data's been invisible. We underwrite with it either way.

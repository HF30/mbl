Can You Build a Sixplex in Spadina–Fort York? Ward 10

Spadina–Fort York (Ward 10) is one of the nine Toronto wards where up to six residential units are permitted as-of-right, under Zoning By-law 654-2025 and Official Plan Amendment 818 (June 2025). Being in the ward gets a lot to the starting line. Whether six units actually fit — and whether the project pencils — is decided by the specific lot, not the ward.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments · one of a nine-part series, one post per ward.

What the ward covers

Ward 10 is a large downtown-and-waterfront ward covering Toronto’s southwest core. It runs from the Liberty Village / Trinity Bellwoods area in the west, south along the entire central waterfront and out to the Toronto Islands, east to the Don River and Port Lands, and north into the downtown core to around Dundas Street, Bay Street and The Esplanade. The ward takes in CityPlace, Fort York (Garrison Common), Harbourfront, King West / Entertainment District, Bathurst Quay, Niagara, and Wellington Place, along with parts of Chinatown, Grange Park, Alexandra Park, the Financial District fringe, St. Lawrence, the Distillery District and the Toronto Islands. Ward lines follow specific streets and get redrawn from time to time, so treat any neighbourhood list as a guide, not a survey — the City publishes the official ward boundaries on toronto.ca.

Neighbourhoods & built form

The character changes street to street, and the built form is what matters most for a sixplex — it’s the raw material you’re working with, and the heritage-character pockets are where the “does it fit” question gets hardest.

  • CityPlace. Master-planned high-rise condo district on the former Railway Lands; almost entirely 5+ storey towers, essentially no ground-related stock.
  • Fort York / Garrison Common. Newer mid- and high-rise condominiums built around the historic Fort York National Historic Site; predominantly apartment towers.
  • Harbourfront. Waterfront corridor of condo towers along Queens Quay beside the Martin Goodman Trail and lake; overwhelmingly high-rise.
  • King West / Entertainment District. Mix of converted red-brick warehouse (“hard loft”) buildings, mid-rise and towers; scattered Victorian-era side-street pockets, including designated 1880s row houses on Draper Street.
  • Niagara / Wellington Place. One of the ward’s few concentrations of older low-rise and Victorian/row-house fabric alongside newer mid-rise condos; the most relevant pocket for ground-related feasibility.
  • Bathurst Quay. Waterfront community near Billy Bishop airport and Little Norway Park, blending low-rise co-op and townhouse housing with condo buildings.

Getting around

Served by TTC Line 1 (Yonge–University) with Union, St. Andrew, Osgoode, St. Patrick and Spadina stations along or just north of the ward, plus GO Transit and the UP Express at Union. Dense streetcar coverage includes the 504 King, 509 Harbourfront, 510 Spadina and 511 Bathurst routes. The Gardiner Expressway and Lake Shore Boulevard run through the waterfront, and Bathurst Street is a key north–south arterial.

Downtown access & lifestyle

A downtown-core and waterfront location with the Harbourfront Centre, Queens Quay promenade and the Martin Goodman Trail on the lake; the King West and Entertainment District nightlife, theatres, restaurants and clubs; and major venues such as Rogers Centre and Scotiabank Arena nearby. The area hosts the annual Toronto International Film Festival (TIFF) in September. This concentration of jobs, transit and amenities underpins sustained rental demand from young professionals.

Schools & families

Public schools are operated by the Toronto District School Board (TDSB) and Catholic schools by the Toronto Catholic District School Board (TCDSB); the ward includes downtown elementary options such as Waterfront School and Ryerson Community School. Specific catchment assignment depends on the exact address and should be confirmed via the TDSB/TCDSB school-locator tools.

Figures are ward-wide 2021 Census counts (income is for 2020), published by City Planning. They describe the ward; they don’t rate it. Every lot inside the ward is different.

Who lives here

The age profile is strongly young-professional. Residents aged 20–29 make up 28.9% (versus 15.5% citywide) and those 30–39 make up 31.9% (versus 16.6%), together about 61% of the population. Children 0–9 are just 5.3% (versus 9.1%) and youth 10–19 are 3.2% (versus 9.6%). The demographic dependency ratio is 17 dependants per 100 working-age residents, far below Toronto’s 44, consistent with a household base dominated by working-age singles and couples.

What that means for a sixplex — and what it doesn’t

A ward that is mostly ground-related, older housing is, in the abstract, the kind of place the six-unit framework was written for: detached, semi-detached, and row houses on residential lots. That is the raw material. It is also exactly where the real questions begin, because none of the ward-level numbers above can tell you what a specific lot will carry.

Being in Ward 10 means the six-unit framework may apply to a lot here. It does not mean six units fit, or that the building is otherwise compliant or pencils. The same lot-by-lot gates apply here as everywhere:

  • Lot geometry. Frontage and depth, minus setbacks, set the buildable envelope. On a narrow lot the sixth unit often has to become a rear suite — a different legal and financing object than six units in one building.
  • Overlays, heritage, and specific zoning. The ward is the boundary; the actual zoning, heritage designations, and overlays run lot by lot and can quietly cap what you build.
  • Trees, easements, servicing. A protected tree, a registered easement, or a servicing constraint can constrain the design, add cost, or reduce the unit count — long before price is the issue.
  • What’s in the ground. On any lot with an existing or former structure, environmental status is unknown until it’s investigated. Real cost, real gate.

A qualified zoning review on the specific lot is step one, and if the review and the ward map ever disagree, the review wins.

How you’d actually evaluate a lot here

The honest sequence is the same in every one of the nine wards: confirm the lot can carry six units on paper (geometry), confirm the market supports the rents (closed-lease evidence, not asking prices), and confirm the numbers pencil at a price you can actually pay — before you fall in love with an address. It runs lot by lot, not ward by ward. That discipline is the whole difference between a buildable sixplex and an expensive lesson.

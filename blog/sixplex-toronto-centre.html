Can You Build a Sixplex in Toronto Centre? Ward 13

Toronto Centre (Ward 13) is one of the nine Toronto wards where up to six residential units are permitted as-of-right, under Zoning By-law 654-2025 and Official Plan Amendment 818 (June 2025). Being in the ward gets a lot to the starting line. Whether six units actually fit — and whether the project pencils — is decided by the specific lot, not the ward.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments · one of a nine-part series, one post per ward.

What the ward covers

Downtown east. Bounded roughly by Bay Street, Yonge Street and Dundas Street West on the west; The Esplanade and Mill Street on the south; the Don River / Bayview Avenue / DVP on the east; and Rosedale Valley Road, Bloor Street East, Mount Pleasant Road and Charles Street on the north. The ward takes in Cabbagetown, St. James Town, Regent Park, Church and Wellesley (the Village), Moss Park, Corktown, the Distillery District, the St. Lawrence Market area, the Garden District, and part of the downtown core. Ward lines follow specific streets and get redrawn from time to time, so treat any neighbourhood list as a guide, not a survey — the City publishes the official ward boundaries on toronto.ca.

Neighbourhoods & built form

The character changes street to street, and the built form is what matters most for a sixplex — it’s the raw material you’re working with, and the heritage-character pockets are where the “does it fit” question gets hardest.

  • Cabbagetown. Largely heritage low-rise; often described as the largest preserved continuous area of Victorian houses in North America, most built 1860–1895. This is where ground-related, plex-convertible stock concentrates.
  • St. James Town. High-density apartment-tower cluster (1960s–70s); very tall built form, effectively no ground-related infill potential.
  • Regent Park. Master-planned mixed-income redevelopment of mid/high-rise blocks; new-build towers rather than lot-scale housing.
  • Church and Wellesley (the Village). Mix of mid/high-rise apartments and some older low-rise; dense, transit-adjacent core area.
  • Corktown / Distillery District. Corktown retains pockets of 19th-century low-rise/rowhouse stock near Parliament and the Don; the Distillery District is a heritage-industrial precinct flanked by new condo towers.
  • Moss Park / Garden District. Central-downtown mix of towers, social housing and older low-rise; predominantly apartment built form.

Getting around

Very transit-rich. Line 1 (Yonge) stations sit along the western edge at Yonge Street — College, Wellesley and Dundas. Line 2 (Bloor-Danforth) runs the northern edge with Sherbourne and Castle Frank stations; Bloor-Yonge is at the northwest corner. Streetcar service includes the 506 Carlton, 505 Dundas and 504 King, plus Yonge/Jarvis/Sherbourne/Parliament bus routes. Riders in interior areas such as Cabbagetown and Regent Park rely more on streetcars and buses than on the subway.

Downtown access & lifestyle

Contains much of Toronto's downtown core: Church-Wellesley Village (Toronto's historic 2SLGBTQ+ district and the focal point of Pride Toronto, held each June and drawing very large crowds), Cabbagetown's Victorian streets and independent shops, the Distillery District's pedestrian heritage precinct, and the St. Lawrence Market area — plus extensive restaurants and nightlife. Annual events include Pride Toronto and the Cabbagetown Festival, a long-running September street festival on Parliament and Carlton. Concentrated amenities and jobs support strong rental demand.

Schools & families

Served by both the Toronto District School Board (TDSB) and Toronto Catholic District School Board (TCDSB); catchments are assigned by home address. As a dense, mixed-income downtown ward, its public schools do not stand out on province-wide EQAO rankings, and school choice here is driven more by proximity, French-immersion/specialty programs and private options than by catchment reputation. Families should confirm the specific catchment school by address with the TDSB and TCDSB.

Who lives here

A young, renter-dominant, income-diverse population. Median age is 34.8. The 20–29 cohort is 25.2% (Toronto 15.5%) and 30–39 is 23.7% (Toronto 16.6%), so roughly half of residents are in their 20s and 30s. Children 0–9 are just 5.9% (Toronto 9.1%) and seniors 65+ about 11% (Toronto ~17%). The demographic dependency ratio is 23 dependants per 100 working-age vs 44 citywide, and income spans a notable low-income share alongside high earners.

What that means for a sixplex — and what it doesn’t

A ward that is mostly ground-related, older housing is, in the abstract, the kind of place the six-unit framework was written for: detached, semi-detached, and row houses on residential lots. That is the raw material. It is also exactly where the real questions begin, because none of the ward-level numbers above can tell you what a specific lot will carry.

Being in Ward 13 means the six-unit framework may apply to a lot here. It does not mean six units fit, or that the building is otherwise compliant or pencils. The same lot-by-lot gates apply here as everywhere:

  • Lot geometry. Frontage and depth, minus setbacks, set the buildable envelope. On a narrow lot the sixth unit often has to become a rear suite — a different legal and financing object than six units in one building.
  • Overlays, heritage, and specific zoning. The ward is the boundary; the actual zoning, heritage designations, and overlays run lot by lot and can quietly cap what you build.
  • Trees, easements, servicing. A protected tree, a registered easement, or a servicing constraint can constrain the design, add cost, or reduce the unit count — long before price is the issue.
  • What’s in the ground. On any lot with an existing or former structure, environmental status is unknown until it’s investigated. Real cost, real gate.

A qualified zoning review on the specific lot is step one, and if the review and the ward map ever disagree, the review wins.

How you’d actually evaluate a lot here

The honest sequence is the same in every one of the nine wards: confirm the lot can carry six units on paper (geometry), confirm the market supports the rents (closed-lease evidence, not asking prices), and confirm the numbers pencil at a price you can actually pay — before you fall in love with an address. It runs lot by lot, not ward by ward. That discipline is the whole difference between a buildable sixplex and an expensive lesson.

Can You Build a Sixplex in Parkdale–High Park? Ward 4

Parkdale–High Park (Ward 4) is one of the nine Toronto wards where up to six residential units are permitted as-of-right, under Zoning By-law 654-2025 and Official Plan Amendment 818 (June 2025). Being in the ward gets a lot to the starting line. Whether six units actually fit — and whether the project pencils — is decided by the specific lot, not the ward.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments · one of a nine-part series, one post per ward.

What the ward covers

Ward 4 runs along Toronto’s west end, bounded by the Humber River and a rail corridor to the west and north, Dufferin Street and the Gardiner Expressway area to the east, and Lake Ontario to the south, wrapping around High Park. The ward takes in South Parkdale, Roncesvalles Village, High Park North, Swansea, Bloor West Village, High Park-Swansea, and the Sunnyside waterfront. Ward lines follow specific streets and get redrawn from time to time, so treat any neighbourhood list as a guide, not a survey — the City publishes the official ward boundaries on toronto.ca.

Neighbourhoods & built form

The character changes street to street, and the built form is what matters most for a sixplex — it’s the raw material you’re working with, and the heritage-character pockets are where the “does it fit” question gets hardest.

  • South Parkdale. Large late-Victorian and Edwardian houses, many long ago subdivided into rooming houses and rental flats, mixed with older low-rise and high-rise apartment blocks near the lakeshore.
  • Roncesvalles Village. Two- and three-storey Victorian and Edwardian semis and rowhouses dating from the 1880s onward, originally built for the city’s working and merchant classes.
  • High Park North. A mix of detached and semi-detached houses alongside mid-rise and high-rise rental apartment buildings clustered near Bloor Street and the park.
  • Swansea. Detached and semi-detached homes in Victorian, Edwardian and Tudor styles on the slopes running toward the Humber River and the lake.
  • Bloor West Village. Established residential streets of detached and semi-detached houses framing a pedestrian-oriented retail main street along Bloor Street West.

Getting around

The ward sits on Line 2 Bloor–Danforth, with High Park, Keele and Runnymede stations along Bloor Street West and Dundas West station at its eastern edge. Surface routes include the 504 King, 501 Queen, 505 Dundas and 506 Carlton streetcars, the last looping at the High Park Loop. Regional service runs from the Bloor GO station and the adjacent UP Express stop on the Kitchener corridor, linking the area to Union Station and Pearson Airport. Major roads include Bloor Street West, Queen Street West, King Street West, Lake Shore Boulevard, Roncesvalles Avenue, Parkside Drive and Dufferin Street, with the Gardiner Expressway running along the southern edge.

Downtown access & lifestyle

The ward lies immediately west of downtown, roughly a 15-to-20-minute transit ride from the core, and is anchored by High Park, the city’s largest park, whose spring cherry-blossom bloom draws visitors from across the region. Retail and restaurant strips run along Roncesvalles Avenue, Bloor West Village and the Queen Street West stretch through Parkdale, while the Martin Goodman Trail, Sunnyside Beach and Marilyn Bell Park line the Lake Ontario waterfront to the south. The annual Roncesvalles Polish Festival, one of North America’s largest, closes the avenue each September. This combination of parkland, walkable main streets, waterfront access and rapid transit supports steady rental demand across the ward.

Schools & families

The ward is served by both the Toronto District School Board (TDSB) and the Toronto Catholic District School Board (TCDSB), with catchments assigned by home address. Local TDSB public elementary schools include Swansea Junior and Senior Public School, Runnymede Junior and Senior Public School, Fern Avenue Junior and Senior Public School and Howard Junior Public School, with Parkdale Collegiate Institute among the secondary options. Families should confirm the specific catchment for any address, as boundaries vary block to block.

Who lives here

Census data points to a working-age, professional-leaning population: the median age is 40.0, close to the Toronto figure of 39.6, and the largest age cohort is the 30-to-39 group at 19.6%, well above the city’s 16.6%, with the 40-to-49 band (15.7%) also above the Toronto average. By contrast, residents in their twenties are underrepresented, at 11.7% versus 15.6% city-wide, while young children aged 0-9 (9.3%) sit just above the Toronto share (9.1%). At 68.3 people per hectare the ward is considerably denser than Toronto’s 43.4, and 56% of households rent, above the city’s 48%.

What that means for a sixplex — and what it doesn’t

A ward that is mostly ground-related, older housing is, in the abstract, the kind of place the six-unit framework was written for: detached, semi-detached, and row houses on residential lots. That is the raw material. It is also exactly where the real questions begin, because none of the ward-level numbers above can tell you what a specific lot will carry.

Being in Ward 4 means the six-unit framework may apply to a lot here. It does not mean six units fit, or that the building is otherwise compliant or pencils. The same lot-by-lot gates apply here as everywhere:

  • Lot geometry. Frontage and depth, minus setbacks, set the buildable envelope. On a narrow lot the sixth unit often has to become a rear suite — a different legal and financing object than six units in one building.
  • Overlays, heritage, and specific zoning. The ward is the boundary; the actual zoning, heritage designations, and overlays run lot by lot and can quietly cap what you build.
  • Trees, easements, servicing. A protected tree, a registered easement, or a servicing constraint can constrain the design, add cost, or reduce the unit count — long before price is the issue.
  • What’s in the ground. On any lot with an existing or former structure, environmental status is unknown until it’s investigated. Real cost, real gate.

A qualified zoning review on the specific lot is step one, and if the review and the ward map ever disagree, the review wins.

How you’d actually evaluate a lot here

The honest sequence is the same in every one of the nine wards: confirm the lot can carry six units on paper (geometry), confirm the market supports the rents (closed-lease evidence, not asking prices), and confirm the numbers pencil at a price you can actually pay — before you fall in love with an address. It runs lot by lot, not ward by ward. That discipline is the whole difference between a buildable sixplex and an expensive lesson.

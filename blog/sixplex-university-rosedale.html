Can You Build a Sixplex in University–Rosedale? Ward 11

University–Rosedale (Ward 11) is one of the nine Toronto wards where up to six residential units are permitted as-of-right, under Zoning By-law 654-2025 and Official Plan Amendment 818 (June 2025). Being in the ward gets a lot to the starting line. Whether six units actually fit — and whether the project pencils — is decided by the specific lot, not the ward.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments · one of a nine-part series, one post per ward.

What the ward covers

A downtown/midtown ward with boundaries running roughly Ossington Avenue on the west; Bayview Avenue, the Don River and Rosedale Valley Road on the east; College/Dundas Streets, Charles Street and Yonge Street on the south; and the Canadian Pacific Railway tracks, Mount Pleasant Cemetery and Moore Park Ravine on the north. Bloor Street runs through the ward's midsection. The ward takes in the Annex, Yorkville, and Rosedale, plus University (the University of Toronto campus) and the Discovery District, with Harbord Village, Seaton Village, and the College Street stretch of Little Italy within or adjacent to the ward. Ward lines follow specific streets and get redrawn from time to time, so treat any neighbourhood list as a guide, not a survey — the City publishes the official ward boundaries on toronto.ca.

Neighbourhoods & built form

The character changes street to street, and the built form is what matters most for a sixplex — it’s the raw material you’re working with, and the heritage-character pockets are where the “does it fit” question gets hardest.

  • The Annex. Dense grid of late-19th-century grand brick-and-stone Victorian and Edwardian houses (many large detached and semi-detached), long subdivided into student rentals, rooming houses and multi-unit conversions near U of T; a mix directly relevant to multiplex feasibility.
  • Rosedale. Large detached estate homes on wide lots and curving streets; among Toronto's highest-income areas by census income data, with heritage character and low-density built form that constrains as-of-right intensification.
  • Yorkville. Former Victorian village core now a high-rise and mid-rise district of condos, retail and hotels alongside surviving low-rise houses; ground-related lots are scarce and land values high.
  • University / Discovery District. University of Toronto campus and adjacent institutional/research and hospital blocks; purpose-built and converted student housing dominates, driving steady rental demand.
  • Harbord Village. Smaller-scale Victorian rowhouse and semi-detached streets south of Bloor near U of T; a residential pocket of narrower ground-related lots.
  • Little Italy (College St). Two- and three-storey mixed-use main street with residential above retail plus side-street houses along and near College Street toward the ward's western edge.

Getting around

Very transit-rich. Line 2 (Bloor–Danforth) runs east–west through the ward with stations including Bathurst, Spadina, St George and Bay. Line 1 (Yonge–University–Spadina) serves the ward at St George and Spadina (both interchange stations with Line 2), Dupont, Museum and Queen's Park on the University leg, and Rosedale on the Yonge leg. Streetcar routes include the 506 Carlton along College Street, 510 Spadina, and 511 Bathurst; numerous bus routes supplement.

Downtown access & lifestyle

Downtown- and midtown-adjacent, walkable and amenity-dense. Yorkville offers concentrated retail and dining; Bloor Street is a major shopping and transit spine. The University of Toronto campus, the Royal Ontario Museum and Queen's Park anchor the ward. Ravine and park systems include the Rosedale Valley and Moore Park Ravine on the eastern/northern edges. The Taste of Little Italy street festival on College Street (a long-running free June event) is a recurring neighbourhood draw. These features, together with the university and hospital/research employment, underpin sustained rental demand.

Schools & families

Public schools are run by the Toronto District School Board (TDSB). Jesse Ketchum Junior and Senior Public School (JK–8, at 61 Davenport Rd near Bay Street/Yorkville) serves the Yorkville/Annex area. The University of Toronto campus lies within the ward, and University of Toronto Schools (UTS, 371 Bloor St W) is an independent, university-affiliated school rather than a public/EQAO-ranked TDSB option. Specific EQAO scores and any provincial ranking for Jesse Ketchum were not verified here, so no performance claim is made; confirm the catchment school for any address with the TDSB.

Who lives here

Data-backed and bimodal. Census figures show a large young-adult concentration (22.6% aged 20–29 vs 15.5% citywide) tied to the University of Toronto, alongside high-income households (18.9% earning $200,000+; average household income $174,800) concentrated in areas like Rosedale and Yorkville. Children aged 0–9 are underrepresented (5.9% vs 9.1% citywide) and the demographic dependency ratio is low (36 dependants per 100 working-age vs 44 citywide), consistent with a student- and professional-heavy, family-light population. The income distribution is barbell-shaped, with roughly 10% of households under $20,000 coexisting with the high-income top end.

What that means for a sixplex — and what it doesn’t

A ward that is mostly ground-related, older housing is, in the abstract, the kind of place the six-unit framework was written for: detached, semi-detached, and row houses on residential lots. That is the raw material. It is also exactly where the real questions begin, because none of the ward-level numbers above can tell you what a specific lot will carry.

Being in Ward 11 means the six-unit framework may apply to a lot here. It does not mean six units fit, or that the building is otherwise compliant or pencils. The same lot-by-lot gates apply here as everywhere:

  • Lot geometry. Frontage and depth, minus setbacks, set the buildable envelope. On a narrow lot the sixth unit often has to become a rear suite — a different legal and financing object than six units in one building.
  • Overlays, heritage, and specific zoning. The ward is the boundary; the actual zoning, heritage designations, and overlays run lot by lot and can quietly cap what you build.
  • Trees, easements, servicing. A protected tree, a registered easement, or a servicing constraint can constrain the design, add cost, or reduce the unit count — long before price is the issue.
  • What’s in the ground. On any lot with an existing or former structure, environmental status is unknown until it’s investigated. Real cost, real gate.

A qualified zoning review on the specific lot is step one, and if the review and the ward map ever disagree, the review wins.

How you’d actually evaluate a lot here

The honest sequence is the same in every one of the nine wards: confirm the lot can carry six units on paper (geometry), confirm the market supports the rents (closed-lease evidence, not asking prices), and confirm the numbers pencil at a price you can actually pay — before you fall in love with an address. It runs lot by lot, not ward by ward. That discipline is the whole difference between a buildable sixplex and an expensive lesson.

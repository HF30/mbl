Deal Autopsy: Why We Passed on an Entire District

The short answer: we measured 54 closed 2026 leases across one west-end district before bidding on anything, found closed-unit medians of about $3,000 for 3-beds and $2,525 for 2-beds, calculated that those rents support roughly $500–600k of land under our program — and the district's live listings were asking $819k to $1.15M. Then zoning delivered the second no: most of the district caps at four units, not six. We passed on everything. This is what discipline actually looks like in practice.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments Research · First in a monthly series — real evaluations, real numbers, deals we didn't do.

Why publish deals we passed on?

Every developer publishes their wins. Nobody shows you the discipline — the properties that looked cheap and weren't. But the pass is the system: a buy box only means something if it says no most of the time. So each month we'll autopsy a real evaluation from our pipeline: what the data said, what the zoning said, and why the answer was no. (Live targets stay confidential — autopsies are published only after we've walked away.)

The autopsy

The setup: a west-end district full of wide lots at seemingly cheap per-foot prices — $224–298 per square foot of land, roughly half what premium east-end pockets trade at. On the surface: a value hunter's dream.

Step one — measure the rents. Before bidding a dollar we pulled every 2026 closed lease in the district: 54 of them. Closed-unit medians: ~$3,000 for 3-bedrooms, ~$2,525 for 2-bedrooms. Solid rents — but 25–40% below the east-end pockets where our buildings pencil.

Step two — solve for the land. A six-unit building's finished value is its rent roll capitalized; back out construction (fixed-price), soft costs, financing and the CMHC premium, and what's left is the most the land can be worth to a rental developer. At this district's closed rents: roughly $500–600k per lot.

Step three — compare to the asks. Live listings: $819k to $1.15M. The market was pricing these lots for custom homes and land banking — $250k to $500k above what rental economics support. Cheap-looking per-foot is not cheap against the rents that have to service the debt.

Step four — the zoning check that ended it. Only a sliver of the district sits in a ward where sixplexes are as-of-right. The rest caps at four units unless the ward opts in — and a fourplex on an $850k lot is a different (worse) business. Wrong rents and wrong zoning: pass on the entire district, put it on the watch list for repricing.

What this autopsy teaches

  1. Per-foot land price is a trap metric. Land is only cheap relative to the rents it can produce. The cheapest-looking district in our dataset failed underwriting by the widest margin.
  2. Measure before you tour. Fifty-four closed leases took the machine an afternoon and cost nothing. One mispriced lot would have cost six figures.
  3. Zoning is binary. The nine-ward map isn't a detail — it's the difference between a sixplex and a fourplex, which is often the difference between a deal and a donation.
  4. A pass is a result. The watch list exists because districts reprice. When this one's asks fall 30%, we'll be the first call.

Frequently asked questions

Which district? We publish the analysis, not the coordinates — sellers there don't deserve a public mark-down from us, and buyers can run the same math anywhere with closed data.

Would you ever buy there? At land prices the rents support — yes, parts of it, where the zoning works. That's the point of a formula: the answer changes when the numbers do.

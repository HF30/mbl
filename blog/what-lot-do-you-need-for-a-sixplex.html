What Lot Do You Need for a Sixplex in Toronto? The Frontage Math Nobody Does

The short answer: in the nine wards, six-unit use may be permitted as-of-right — but whether six compliant, profitable units actually fit is lot-, zone- and design-specific, and the building you can fit changes materially around 35 and 44 feet of frontage. "Use permitted" and "six units fit" are different questions; conflating them is the most expensive mistake in this niche.

Updated August 2026 · MBL Developments

The math that governs everything

The worked example below uses typical low-rise residential-zone standards under Toronto's multiplex framework (By-law 654-2025); your lot's actual zone, overlays and conditions govern — a zoning review is step one, not a formality:

  • Side setbacks commonly take ~0.9m off each side — a 30-ft lot leaves ~24 ft of buildable width
  • Building depth commonly caps around 19m (~62 ft) from the front wall — a deep lot doesn't help the main building past that
  • Height — three storeys is the workable default; the framework's added height is conditional, and a true fourth storey typically becomes a variance/design battle. Most viable schemes are 3 storeys plus a lower level

Multiply it out: a 30-ft lot yields a floor plate of roughly 1,500 sq ft per level — four levels, ~5,900 sq ft of building.

Why frontage decides your unit mix — and your income

Here's the part nobody runs: six units across four levels means two levels must hold two units each. On a 1,500 sq ft plate, any floor you split produces one decent unit and one small one — and small units are where rent rolls die (a 1-bedroom earns $1,500–2,000/month less than a 3-bedroom on nearly the same footprint).

  • Under ~35 ft of frontage: the main building carries full-floor units on the upper levels, splits below — and the highest-rent configuration usually becomes five units in the main building plus one rear suite (laneway or garden suite) for six total. Note that's one specific legal configuration: main-plus-rear-suite affects financing treatment, permit sequencing and fees differently than six-in-main, so confirm the configuration with your architect and lender early. The 140-ft-deep lots common in east Toronto handle the rear suite beautifully.
  • ~44 ft and up: the plate reaches ~2,000+ sq ft per level — two units per floor, both full-sized, all in one building. Simpler build, same rent roll.

Same permitted use, same neighbourhood — but the 44-ft lot builds a fundamentally simpler project. That's worth real money, and almost nobody prices it. (Frontage isn't the whole story either: zone standards, angular planes, landscaping and soft-landscaping minimums, servicing capacity, access, parking and bike requirements, tree protection and — often — a minor variance all shape what actually gets approved.)

What else the lot must clear

  • Trees: one protected tree in the building envelope can redesign your project or stall demolition for months. Survey before you waive conditions.
  • Easements and mutual drives: shared driveways and rights-of-way can kill a footprint outright. Title review is not optional.
  • Lane access: determines whether the rear suite is a laneway suite (needs the lane) or a garden suite (doesn't), and where parking lives.
  • Services and grade: older streets sometimes need service upgrades; a favourable grade makes lower-level units brighter and more rentable.

Frequently asked questions

Can you build a sixplex on a 25-foot lot? Legally, often yes. Practically, the plate gets tight and the unit mix compresses toward smaller, lower-rent units. The economics need careful modelling — this is exactly where per-lot underwriting matters most.

Is a corner lot better? Sometimes — corner exposure can improve light and access, but corner lots also carry their own setback and streetscape rules. Case-by-case; model before paying a premium.

Does a deeper lot mean a bigger building? Not for the main structure (the ~19m depth cap governs), but depth is what makes a rear suite comfortable — and the rear suite is often where the best unit in the project lives.
